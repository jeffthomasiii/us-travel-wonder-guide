# United States Travel & Wonder Guide — Editorial Scope

The guide is intentionally broader than a national-park directory and broader than a 50-state checklist.

Its geographic scope is the **50 states, Washington, D.C., and the five inhabited U.S. territories: Puerto Rico, U.S. Virgin Islands, Guam, Northern Mariana Islands, and American Samoa.** These are treated as primary browse areas because they are places people can genuinely travel to and explore as part of the United States travel sphere.

The U.S. Minor Outlying Islands are not part of the ordinary browse model because most are remote, uninhabited, protected, military-controlled, or otherwise unsuitable as general travel destinations. They can be considered individually later if a real editorial use case emerges.

## Geographic hierarchy

The top-level area is the jurisdiction a traveler browses. Individual Wonders can then be islands, cities, parks, coastlines, caves, roads, neighborhoods, monuments, forests, cultural landscapes, or other places within that area.

For example, **Catalina Island belongs under California**, while **El Yunque belongs under Puerto Rico**. Islands and other subregions do not become peer top-level areas simply because they are geographically distinct.

## National parks are a subset, not the organizing principle

National parks absolutely belong here, and the long-term inventory should include all U.S. national parks because they qualify on merit. But they are only one subset of the places this guide is meant to help people discover. Excellent dedicated national-park resources already exist; this project's value is in putting national parks alongside many other places that can justify a trip, reshape a route, or reveal something extraordinary about an area.

Potential Wonders can include national parks and other NPS units, state parks, forests, wildlife refuges, BLM lands, wilderness, scenic roads, coastlines, beaches, islands, lakes, rivers, waterfalls, caves, mountains, deserts, gardens, dark-sky locations, wildlife destinations, historic and cultural places, architecture, engineering landmarks, and smaller regional or roadside places that are genuinely worth the detour.

The guide should not privilege a destination merely because it is federally designated or widely marketed.

## Editorial test

A candidate belongs when the place itself gives a traveler a compelling reason to see it and fits one of the guide's three trip-shaping roles:

- **★ Signature Wonder** — the one place that best makes the case for its state, district, or territory.
- **◆ Worth Traveling For** — strong enough to justify building part of a trip around it.
- **○ Worth the Detour** — worth changing or extending a route to experience when nearby.

Designation, popularity, and visitor count are evidence to consider, not automatic qualification.

## Diversity guardrail for expansion

Before an area is considered reasonably developed, review whether its entries represent the actual range of wonder available there. That may include federal and state/local land, urban and rural places, natural and cultural destinations, highly accessible stops and remote destinations, famous anchors and lesser-known discoveries.

Do not force diversity numerically, but do not let the easiest-to-research category become the de facto scope of the guide.
